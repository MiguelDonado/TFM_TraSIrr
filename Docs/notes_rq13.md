Your distance_to_min catch is exactly right, and worth stating precisely. distance_to_min = (mean_tt - min_mean_tt) / min_mean_tt (confirmed in the code, line 393-394) uses the population-pooled shortest route's mean as both reference point and denominator. x_r = (AC - PC_r)/AC uses a completely different reference: AC, a per-agent, gamma-weighted average over all the routes that agent has personally experienced recently — including the bad ones. Since AC pools in worse routes too, AC ≥ min_mean_tt in general, especially early in learning or under high memory (where a bad-route trial from episode 5 still drags on AC at episode 100). That means distance_to_min systematically understates how large the real x_r margins looked to an agent relative to its own (inflated) AC — not just "not exactly comparable," but biased in a specific, explainable direction. That's a good thing to note in the write-up if you use this table alongside the plot.

And that resolves the thing I got slightly wrong last time: I framed the tau=0.05 vs tau=0.2 reversal as "the dead zone doesn't engage at 0.05, so you're just seeing plain BM noise dynamics." Your framing is cleaner — it's the same mechanism throughout, just below or above the point where it matters. The margin is smaller under memory=1 at every tau, but it's a stable number — so at tau=0.05 it's still comfortably clear of the threshold (differentiation happens, and happens decisively, precisely because it's stable rather than wobbling around the boundary). At tau=0.2/0.4, that same shrunk-but-stable margin now sits inside the threshold, so it gets zeroed out consistently, not intermittently. Under low memory, the margin is larger to begin with (tighter AC) — so it takes a bigger tau before the same routes stop being distinguishable, which is exactly why the low-memory panels keep separating routes even at tau=0.2 where memory=1 has already gone flat.

flow-differentiation-failure and R-gap-harm respond to gap size in opposite directions, so they don't validate each other the way I was implicitly assuming.

- Near-tied OD (small true gap, like plot 3's): easy for a moderate/high tau to swallow (frequent non-differentiation), but cheap when it happens — since all the candidate routes are already close to optimal, splitting flow among them instead of concentrating on the single cheapest one barely moves R-gap. Low differentiation, low cost.
- Well-separated OD (large true gap): hard for tau to swallow at all — you need a genuinely large tau before the dead zone even reaches it — but expensive when it finally does, since ignoring a route that's substantially better is a real efficiency loss, not a rounding error. Rare differentiation failure, high cost when it occurs.
