## Congestion

### Static assignment model

- Vehicles are treated as continuous flow
- Links have cost functions (BPR functions)
- A link can carry arbitrarily large flows as long as the cost function is evaluated

### Microsimulator

- Every vehicle is simulated individually
- A 100 m, one-lane road can physically hold only about:
- Vehicle length ≈ 5 m
- Gap ≈ 2.5 m
- Total space ≈ 7.5 m/vehicle
  So the road stores roughly 13 vehicles.
  Even with movement, a one-lane urban road typically has a capacity on the order of **1,500–2,000 vehicles/hour** under ideal conditions.
- Congestion only appears when the demand exceeds the discharge capacity of some bottleneck
- Common bottlenecks:
  - Traffic lights
  - Priority junctions (vehicles yield)
  - Lane drops
  - Merges
  - Turns

## Network and demand gotchas

**¿Why the R-gap still converges with memory = 0.001?**

It degenerates into a one-day-lookback comparison. 

- **PT for the chosen route**: The just observed travel time gets weight γ⁰=1, and any earlier visit to that same route (even yesterday) gets weight ≤ γ¹ = 0.001  PT_chosen collapses to just today's observation.
- **ET**: same mechanism, but pooled across all routes/days — the day-(t−1) term gets weight 1, day-(t−2) gets weight 0.001, etc. → ET collapses to just yesterday's observed travel time, whatever route that was.
- **stimulus** ≈ normalize(tt(t−1) − tt(t)). Was today's route better than whatever I happened to experience yesterday?"

**Clearly dominant route consequences**

This network clearly has one persistently cheap route (route 0, tt ≈ 112–120) versus the rest (tt ≈ 130–220+, congestion-dependent). Because route 0 is almost always cheaper than whatever was tried the day before, the one-step comparison usually has the correct sign whenever route 0 gets sampled — giving stimulus ≈ +1 and a big reinforcement jump. When a worse route gets sampled after the agent has drifted toward route 0, it looks bad by comparison and gets punished.

And if the route 0 is chosen two consecutive days, probably they would have very similar times, and then if that small difference is negative, nothing will happen, because when normalizing by the biggest loss, there would be other routes with a large loss, and hence stimulus would be very small, and hence probabilities wont be almost updated, and route 0 wont be penalized.

So it's not that low memory is "harmless" — it destroys the model's ability to hold a stable, averaged expectation 

Here it still trends toward the good route because there's a large, mostly-consistent gap between route 0 and the alternatives. If the routes were closer in cost, or cost ranking flipped often due to congestion, this degenerate γ=0.001 regime would likely fail to converge cleanly (much more oscillation, since it's reacting to single noisy samples rather than a stable average).

Basically this reduces to the standard error of the sample mean $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. If the travel time distribution has almost no variability, I dont need much observations to get a good estimate of the mean. If the travel time distribution has variability then I need more observations to get a precise estimate (with low SE). So low memory would work on cases with low variability, but with larger variability will not.

The paper's non-convergence under low ψ: many agents simultaneously chase "yesterday's good route," collectively overload it, which makes it worse the next day, causing everyone to bail simultaneously, then flip back — that's their "cyclical oscillation" (Fig. 18). That effect needs (a) enough shared demand on a route relative to its capacity that switching mass visibly moves its congestion, and (b) route costs close enough together that this back-and-forth can actually flip the ranking.

How it would be with high memory: If today we obtain a bad travel time in A, because our perception about A route is the average over many past travel times on that route, our perceived cost only changes partially. This creates an smoothing effect. The oscillations become less synchronized. If one day one route is bad, with lower memory all change aggresively its probability mass to other routes, and then flow changed a lot. So agents are synchronized. But if higher memory, then their perception about routes, depend a lot on their past travel times and perception about the route, and this bad time that we got today is gonna affect a little bit, hence different agents had different perceptions about the same route A, they would still continue to have different perceptions about the route. With lower memory, because all changed aggresively to a very bad perception, then they are more synchronized.

When a bad route (high memory): Perception small update -> Stimulus is not as negative -> Policies are not updated that aggresively. -> There is no large synchronized movement of demand 

High memory can reduce the magnitude of individual policy updates, which reduces synchronized switching of agents. The probability mass moves less dramatically, so the realized switching fraction is generally smaller and less synchronized.

**Similar route costs consequences**

If there are routes that are close, with low memory it may oscillate between those. Because it will try one, it will get a good time, then it will sample again, then get a good time, then sample again and it happened to be on the other route and it got a good time, then because of the algorithm workings, even if the improvement is very small, it will give a stimulus of 1 to the new route, and be oscillating between routes.

Why it "looks" converged anyway: routes 0, 4, 5, 6, 7 all cluster around similar travel times (~112–135s), while routes 1, 2, 3 (~150–220s) get correctly and permanently abandoned. So mean travel time drops fast and stays flat — but that flat mean is consistent with the flow bouncing around within the competitive tier indefinitely. 

DUE (Wardrop equilibrium) only requires that used routes have equal, minimal cost — it says nothing about which agent uses which of several equal-cost routes, or how that split evolves. Routes 0, 4, 5, 6, 7 in this OD pair all sit around 112–135s — meaning flow reshuffling among them isn't a equilibrium violation, it's exactly what you'd expect at equilibrium when multiple routes are cost-tied (UE doesn't pin down a unique split in that case). R-gap correctly ignores that churn because it's only sensitive to whether people are using routes meaningfully costlier than the cheapest available one.

The condition you actually want is neither of those — you want a network/OD structure where the correct equilibrium flow split is narrow and load-sensitive: routes whose relative cost ordering flips as flow shifts (a real crossover, not a static tie), so that a route being "temporarily favored" by noisy myopic learning becomes measurably worse once enough agents pile onto it. That's what makes R-gap sensitive to γ — because getting the split wrong now has a real, non-negligible cost.
